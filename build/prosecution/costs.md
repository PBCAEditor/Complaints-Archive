If the defendant is convicted, you can ask the magistrates to order them to pay some or all of the reasonable costs of bringing the prosecution. The court decides whether to award costs and how much to award. The purpose of a costs order is to compensate the prosecutor for the cost of the prosecution, not to impose an additional punishment.

You should prepare your costs request before the plea hearing. If the defendant pleads guilty, the court may deal with sentencing and costs immediately. Bring enough copies for the court and the defendant.

A [model costs schedule](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-costs-schedule.docx) is available to download at the end of this page.

## How much should I ask for?

Your claim has two parts: the money you actually spent on the prosecution, and the time you spent on it. Keep a log of both from the start, recording the date, what you did and how long it took, and keep every receipt. A log written up afterwards is much weaker evidence.

### Actual expenses

You can ask the court to consider reasonable expenses which you actually incurred in bringing the prosecution.

Keep receipts or other evidence wherever possible.

These might include:

- postage or other costs of serving the summons and prosecution papers
- necessary printing or copying
- reasonable travel to and from court
- legal advice or representation which you reasonably needed and paid for, if any
- other identifiable expenses which were reasonably necessary for the prosecution

You should not include expenses connected with the wider dispute which were not incurred for the prosecution itself. For example, the cost of making the original section 21 or 22 request, pursuing a complaint, campaigning about the landlord or conducting separate tribunal proceedings should not normally be added simply because some of that material later became evidence in the prosecution.

### Your time

You can also ask the court to pay for the time you spent on the prosecution. The law is not settled on whether a private prosecutor who is not a lawyer can recover their own time as costs in the magistrates' court, and the court may refuse this part of the claim. Show your time as a separate item from your expenses, so the court can decide on it separately.

There is no set rate for this in the criminal courts. This guide suggests the rate the civil courts allow people who represent themselves, currently £24 an hour (Civil Procedure Rules, Practice Direction 46, paragraph 3.4). The High Court used the civil rate for a self-represented private prosecutor in *R (McGill) v Newcastle Magistrates' Court* [2025] EWHC 161 (Admin), although that was a judicial review, not a claim against a convicted defendant.

Count each hour once, in one of two ways:

- time that did not cost you any pay, such as evenings, weekends or paid holiday: claim it at £24 an hour
- time that cost you pay, such as unpaid leave or a lost shift to attend court: claim the pay you actually lost instead, supported by a payslip or a letter from your employer

Only count time reasonably spent on the prosecution itself, from preparing the summons application onwards. That includes preparing court documents, serving papers, and travelling to and waiting at court. It does not include time spent on the original section 21 or 22 request, complaints, tribunal proceedings or campaigning.

Keep the claim in proportion. If the defendant pleaded guilty at the first hearing, a claim for many hours of preparation is likely to be reduced.

The defendant may point to the Crown Prosecution Service's standard figure for a magistrates' court case, currently £85 for an early guilty plea ([cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/costs-annex-1](https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/costs-annex-1)). That figure is an average of the CPS's own staff costs. Your claim is based on what the prosecution actually cost you.

### If the case required substantially more work

Your time log will record any extra work the case required. Sometimes the course of the proceedings requires significant additional work or additional court appearances, and the court will want to know why.

Examples might include:

- the defendant opposing the issue of the summons, requiring additional written submissions or a separate hearing
- the defendant failing to attend a hearing, causing an adjournment and another necessary court appearance
- an additional case-management hearing being required
- substantial work being completed in preparation for trial before the defendant changes its plea
- a trial requiring more than one hearing or sitting day
- an unusual issue raised by the defendant requiring substantial additional preparation

If your claim is larger than a straightforward case would need, you should be able to explain:

- what happened
- what additional work or expense it required
- why that work was reasonably necessary
- how you arrived at the additional amount requested

Where possible, support the explanation with court notices, correspondence, receipts or other records. Additional costs should relate to work reasonably required by the particular circumstances of the case.

The fact that a defendant pleaded not guilty is not, by itself, a reason to increase a costs claim. Claim for the work the case actually required. A defendant must not be penalised through costs merely for exercising the right to defend the case.

## The court has the final say

Under section 18 of the Prosecution of Offences Act 1985, the court may order a convicted defendant to pay the prosecutor such costs as it considers just and reasonable.

The court will consider matters including whether the costs were actually and reasonably incurred, the defendant's ability to pay and the other financial orders being imposed. Costs should not be grossly disproportionate to the fine.

The approach on this page is therefore a suggested way of making a clear and proportionate application, not a guarantee of what the court will award.

## What if the defendant is acquitted?

If the defendant is acquitted in a section 25 prosecution, you should expect to bear your own prosecution costs. Section 18 of the Prosecution of Offences Act 1985 only allows a costs order against a convicted defendant.

The court also has power under section 19 of the same Act to order a private prosecutor to pay the defendant's costs. This does not happen simply because the prosecution failed. But a prosecution brought for an improper purpose, or in which material adverse to the case was not disclosed, may expose the prosecutor to a costs order.

This is one reason why the [main guide](/resources/prosecution/) asks you to consider your motives honestly, disclose material that could assist the defendant, and stop if the case is not there. A prosecution brought and conducted properly should not give rise to a costs order against you, but the possibility exists and you should be aware of it before you begin.
