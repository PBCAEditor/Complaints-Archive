> **This guide concerns the existing sections 21 to 25 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985.** In England they are being replaced in stages under the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. The section 22 and 23 duties, and the offence of breaching them, end on 1 April 2027. The section 21 duty and its offence continue until 1 November 2027 for private landlords and 1 November 2028 for housing associations and other social landlords. After those dates, failures go to the tribunal instead of the criminal courts. Dates for Wales have not yet been set. If the failure you want to prosecute happened before one of these dates, you may still be able to prosecute it within the usual six months, but take advice first.

## Before you start

This guide is a companion to our guide [Requesting service charge information](/resources/guide.html), which covers sections 21 and 22. It assumes you made a valid statutory request, kept the relevant evidence, and the landlord did not comply by the deadline. If you have not yet made the request, start with that guide.

This guide is written for the leaseholder to whom the statutory duty was owed and takes you through the practical steps involved in bringing the prosecution.

It takes you through:

- deciding whether you have a suitable case
- applying for a summons
- serving the court papers
- preparing for the plea hearing and knowing what to do after the plea

The model documents and checklists are Word files you can download and edit. They are listed under [Downloads](#downloads) at the end of this guide, and each is mentioned where you need it.

## What is a section 25 prosecution?

Section 25(1) of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 makes it a criminal offence for a landlord to fail, without reasonable excuse, to perform a duty imposed by sections 21, 22 or 23. It is a summary offence, tried in the magistrates' court. The current maximum penalty is a level 4 fine (£2,500).

Any person may bring a private prosecution. You do not need the permission of the police, the Crown Prosecution Service or any other body. But a private prosecutor has the same obligations as any other prosecutor. A private prosecutor must act fairly. When applying for the summons, you must tell the court about material information that could undermine your application or assist the defendant. If the case is contested, further disclosure duties will apply. This guide explains what you need to do at each stage.

The Director of Public Prosecutions has a statutory power to take over any private prosecution. In practice this is rarely exercised in cases of this kind. If it happens, you step aside and the DPP takes over conduct of the prosecution.

## Can they be prosecuted under section 25?

Most private landlords and housing associations in England can be prosecuted under section 25. Housing associations are not excluded. If your landlord is an English housing association and is a private registered provider of social housing, that does not protect it from prosecution.

In Wales only, registered social landlords are excluded (section 25(3)). This is a different legal category from the English private registered provider of social housing, and exists only in Wales.

Local authorities, National Park authorities and new town corporations are excluded in both England and Wales (sections 25(3) and 26(1)). If your landlord is a council or another public authority, stop and check whether section 25 applies before continuing.

A section 25 prosecution must normally be started within six months of the offence. This means your application for the summons must reach the court within six months. The court does not have to issue the summons within that six-month period. Do not leave the application until the last few days.

**CHECK:** If you are prosecuting an English housing association, make sure its legal status is described correctly in your application. Use "private registered provider of social housing," not "registered social landlord." Courts have confused the two, leading to a summons application being wrongly refused on the basis of an exemption that did not apply. If this happens, point the court to section 25(3), which applies only to Welsh registered social landlords.

## Do I have a suitable case?

There are five questions which will form the backbone of your case for the summons:

- **Who had the duty?** Usually this will be your immediate landlord. If a superior landlord is involved under section 23, stop and check who owed the particular duty before applying. That is the person or organisation to whom you addressed your statutory request. If they acknowledged it, that helps prove they received it. If a managing agent is involved, the duty to comply sits with the landlord, not the agent. For a section 21 request, a request served on the landlord's named agent, or on whoever collects the rent for the landlord, counts as served on the landlord, and the agent must pass it on as soon as possible (section 21(3)). An agent that fails to pass it on can itself be prosecuted.
- **What did they have to do?** The companion guide explains what sections 21 and 22 require. You do not need to repeat that analysis here, but you should be clear about which duty you say was not performed.
- **When did they have to do it by?** The deadline calculated using the companion guide.
- **Did they do it?** If they did not provide what the law required by the deadline, move to the final question.
- **Have they given a reasonable excuse?** This is a matter for trial, not the summons stage. You do not have to disprove every possible excuse. Record any explanation you already know about and consider it fairly. If it plainly accounts for the failure, do not prosecute. If it is disputed, the court can determine it later.

You should also be able to produce the following evidence at minimum:

- Your statutory request under section 21 or section 22 (or both).
- Proof that it was sent to, or received by, the person who actually owed the duty.
- Evidence that the landlord had not provided what was required by the deadline.

Record any explanation the landlord has given. You must not hide information that could assist the defendant or undermine your case.

## Applying for the summons

The application is made on form SP001. Download the current form from GOV.UK:

[https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/application-for-summons-or-warrant-for-arrest-for-an-alleged-offence](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/application-for-summons-or-warrant-for-arrest-for-an-alleged-offence)

Always use the court's own form. We do not reproduce it, because court forms change. Instead, the [model wording for form SP001](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-sp001-wording.docx) shows what a leaseholder might type into each box for a section 25 case.

Start a log now of the time you spend on the prosecution and anything you pay for, and keep the receipts. If the landlord is convicted, the log is the evidence for your costs claim (see [Prosecution costs](/resources/prosecution/costs.html)).

### Where to send the application

Use the GOV.UK court finder to identify a magistrates' court office. A sensible starting point is the court serving the area where the property is located. The court may ultimately list the case at a different magistrates' court.

[https://www.find-court-tribunal.service.gov.uk](https://www.find-court-tribunal.service.gov.uk)

Use the court finder to identify the court office and its contact details. Follow the court office's instructions for sending or delivering the application. Many court offices accept correspondence electronically, but do not assume that a generic enquiries address is the correct address for an application if the court gives different instructions.

The form is largely self-explanatory, but the following points may help:

**Summons, not warrant.** The form covers both summonses and warrants for arrest. You want a summons. Ignore the sections about warrants.

**Consent to prosecute.** The form asks whether you need consent to prosecute. For a section 25 offence, the answer is no.

**Previous applications.** The form asks whether you have applied before for a summons for the same allegation. If you have, for example because an earlier application was refused, answer yes and give the details the form asks for.

**Other proceedings.** The form asks whether any other prosecutor has brought a criminal case against the proposed defendant for the same allegation. Related tribunal or Ombudsman proceedings do not make the answer to this question "yes", although they may still need to be mentioned elsewhere if they are materially relevant to the application.

**Details of the alleged offence.** The form asks you to describe the offence in ordinary language and identify the legislation. The [model wording](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-sp001-wording.docx) shows how to do this for a section 25 case. Keep it short: what the duty was, when the request was made, when the deadline expired, and that the defendant failed to comply.

**Summary of the circumstances.** This is where you set out concisely why you say the offence was committed and indicate what evidence you will rely on. It is not a legal argument. State the facts briefly and identify the key documents.

**The declaration.** Read it carefully before you sign. You are confirming that the allegations are substantially true, the evidence will be available at trial, and the application discloses all material information. The court may ask you to confirm this on oath.

**Do not send the form to the defendant.** The form says this explicitly. The court decides whether and how the defendant is notified.

Submit the form to the court office you have identified with a copy of your core evidence: the statutory request, proof of receipt, and evidence showing what, if anything, the landlord had provided by the deadline.

Do not send a full trial bundle. Attach the documents needed to show the basic offence and any other document that is particularly important to the court's decision. If you have any material the court should know about that could assist the defendant or undermine your case, mention it briefly in the summary of circumstances. Keep the application focused and hold additional evidence for later stages.

## What happens after you apply

The simplest outcome is that the court will issue your summons without further steps. If that happens, continue to the next section.

The court may ask for clarification or additional information in writing before deciding whether to issue the summons. This is normal. A good initial application should address obvious points, but no application can anticipate every question the court might ask. Answer specifically if you can, or take advice before you reply.

### If the court lists an application hearing

The court may decide to hold an application hearing before determining whether to issue the summons. If so, the court may also notify the proposed defendant and give it the opportunity to attend, though the defendant is not obliged to do so. You will be required to attend.

This is not a trial. The court is not deciding whether the defendant is guilty. It is deciding whether there is a proper basis for the summons to issue. Prepare a short written argument answering the questions below and send it to the court in advance. If the court has notified the proposed defendant, send it a copy too. A [model written argument](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-written-argument.docx) is available to download. Bring a copy to court, along with your evidence.

**Is the alleged offence known to law?** Yes. Section 25(1) of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 makes it a summary offence to fail, without reasonable excuse, to perform a duty imposed by sections 21, 22 or 23.

**Is there enough evidence on paper to show the basic offence?** This is your core evidence: the request, proof of receipt, and evidence showing what, if anything, the landlord had provided by the deadline.

**Is the application in time?** You have already calculated this. Confirm the date of the offence and the date of your application on the face of your written argument.

**Are you bringing the prosecution for the proper reason?** The court must be satisfied that you are bringing the prosecution to enforce the statutory obligation, not to gain an advantage in other proceedings, harass the landlord, or achieve something the criminal process is not designed for. For most straightforward cases this will be obvious. If you have parallel proceedings with the same landlord, such as a tribunal case about service charge reasonableness or an Ombudsman complaint, mention them and explain briefly that each concerns a different legal question. If you have corresponded extensively with the landlord, be ready to explain why. It is better to raise these things yourself than to have the court discover them and wonder why you did not.

Reasonable excuse is a question for trial, not for the summons stage. If the court asks about it, explain that any disputed reasonable excuse is a matter to be determined at trial. You do not need to defeat the defendant's explanation at this hearing, and you should not try to argue the trial in advance.

## The summons has been issued

Once the court issues the summons, you are conducting a criminal prosecution. You must monitor your case and comply with court directions. Do not assume that everything will happen automatically. There may be administrative steps between the court deciding to issue the summons and the case being listed for hearing. Check with the court, and follow up if necessary. As an individual private prosecutor you are unlikely to be given access to the court's Common Platform case system, so expect to deal with the court by email.

When you receive the summons:

- Check the defendant's name is correct.
- Check the offence matches what you applied for.
- Record the case number.
- Record the hearing date.

## Serving the summons

The court will normally ask you, as the private prosecutor, to serve the summons on the defendant. If the court says it will serve the summons itself, follow its instructions. The court will tell you when service must be completed. Check the summons and the court's instructions carefully. In practice, you may be required to serve it at least 10 working days before the hearing.

You should serve the Initial Details of the Prosecution Case (covered in the next section) at the same time or shortly after. Serve both together if you can.

### How to serve on a company or organisation

Your packet can be served on a corporation by posting it by first class post to its principal office. Check the defendant's principal office address carefully using reliable sources such as its official website, public register and correspondence. If you are unsure which address is legally appropriate, ask before serving the summons. It is good practice to send one copy by first class post and one copy tracked or signed for. Ask the Post Office for a free certificate of posting for the first class copy. Keep the delivery confirmation as evidence of receipt.

You should not serve the defendant's solicitor. Serve the defendant directly unless the court has given other instructions.

### What to include in your service packet

- A copy of the summons sent by the court. Retain the original.
- Your Initial Details of the Prosecution Case (IDPC) (see next section).
- Your core evidence (the request, proof of receipt, and evidence showing what, if anything, the landlord had provided by the deadline).
- Any witness statement (see the [model witness statement](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-witness-statement.docx)).

### Proving service

A first-class letter is normally treated as served on the second business day after posting, unless something different is shown. If you also send a tracked copy, keep the delivery confirmation as additional evidence.

Once service is complete, email a copy of the service packet to the court, together with your certificate of service and proof of postage or delivery. This helps ensure that the court file contains the same prosecution papers that have been served on the defendant. Use the email address the court has been corresponding with you on, unless the court has told you to use a different address.

A [model certificate of service](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-certificate-of-service.docx) is available to download.

## Initial Details of the Prosecution Case

The court and the defendant need to know, before the plea hearing, what the allegation is and what evidence supports it. This is done through a document called the Initial Details of the Prosecution Case, or IDPC.

Write your IDPC clearly enough that you can also use the factual summary as a speaking prompt at the plea hearing. If the defendant pleads guilty, the court may ask you to outline the facts immediately. You should not need a separate account of the offence.

The IDPC should normally contain:

- the charge
- a short factual summary of the case: when the request was made, what the deadline was, what happened
- what happened afterwards (for example, whether the landlord eventually complied, and when)
- anything particularly relevant to the seriousness of the offence, supported by evidence
- the essential evidence (or a list of it)
- the witnesses you may call (in a straightforward case, you may be the only witness) and your witness statement
- the exhibits
- any explanation by the defendant that is relevant to the plea or sentence
- any previous convictions of the defendant that you know of
- any statement you have made about the effect of the offence on you, if you choose to make one
- any other available document or statement that is important to the plea or sentence

A [model IDPC](/resources/prosecution/downloads/s25-model-idpc.docx) is available to download.

Keep it concise. The purpose is to give the court and the defendant a clear picture of the case, not to argue it. If the case is contested, the IDPC can also help identify early on what is actually in dispute. You do not need to allege dishonesty or bad faith. Prove the smallest case necessary to establish the offence.

You should also prepare a costs schedule before the hearing. If the defendant pleads guilty and the court proceeds immediately to sentence, you may be asked about costs on the spot. The costs schedule is a separate document. Guidance on what costs a private prosecutor can claim, and a model costs schedule, are in [Prosecution costs](/resources/prosecution/costs.html).

## The plea hearing

The hearing date shown on the summons will normally be the plea hearing. This is different from any application hearing you may already have attended before the summons was issued. Arrive at the time stated on the summons and follow the court's instructions for checking in. Several cases may have the same listing time, so your case may not be called immediately.

Have your prosecution papers ready, including your summons, IDPC and core evidence, costs schedule, any court correspondence or directions, and something to make notes with. As you do not know how the defendant will plead, also bring a printed copy of the booklet [If They Plead Not Guilty](/resources/prosecution/if-they-plead-not-guilty.html) and familiarise yourself with it and with [If They Plead Guilty](/resources/prosecution/if-they-plead-guilty.html).

The courtroom will normally have several people in it besides you and the defendant or its representative. Understanding who they are will help you follow what is happening.

**The magistrates.** There will usually be two or three magistrates sitting together, or occasionally a single District Judge. They are the decision-makers. They decide whether the offence is proved, what sentence to impose and whether to award costs. Address them as "Sir," "Madam" or "Your Worships" if there is more than one, or "Sir" or "Madam" if there is a District Judge sitting alone.

**The legal adviser.** Sometimes called the court clerk. This is a qualified lawyer who sits in front of the magistrates. The legal adviser advises them on law and procedure, but does not decide the case. In practice, the legal adviser will often manage the hearing: calling the case, asking for pleas, checking what is agreed, and working through case-management questions. If you are unsure about a procedural point during the hearing, the legal adviser is the person to ask.

**The usher.** The usher manages the courtroom. They will call you in, tell you where to sit, and call the case. If you arrive and are not sure where to go, find the usher.

**The defendant or its representative.** The landlord may attend through a solicitor, barrister, or a representative from the organisation. In a corporate prosecution, a person does not need to appear: the defendant may be represented by its lawyers throughout.

You will normally be asked to stand when addressing the court and when the magistrates enter or leave. Watch what others do and follow the same pattern.

When the case is called, identify yourself as the private prosecutor. The court will deal with the defendant's plea.

If the defendant pleads **guilty**, the court may proceed immediately to sentence and costs. See [If They Plead Guilty](/resources/prosecution/if-they-plead-guilty.html).

If the defendant pleads **not guilty**, the court will usually deal with the arrangements needed for the case to proceed to trial. See [If They Plead Not Guilty](/resources/prosecution/if-they-plead-not-guilty.html).

If the defendant does not attend, or something else unexpected happens, follow the court's directions. If the court raises an issue you do not understand, say so and ask for time to consider it or take advice if necessary.

## Using AI tools

AI tools can be useful for organising documents, checking a chronology or explaining unfamiliar terminology. They should not be treated as the authority on whether you have a criminal case, whether an excuse is legally reasonable, what you must disclose or what you should tell the court. Check important legal points against current legislation, court rules or reliable legal advice.
