Commonhold is the future. Who pays for the past?

By David Wood · Published 23 November 2026

A better ownership model for new flats is welcome. The harder question is what the Bill actually changes for people already living under leasehold.

The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill was introduced on 19 November, the day Grand Theft Auto VI was due out, and the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government marked the occasion with a meme on Facebook and Instagram: leasehold beside the GTA V logo, commonhold beside GTA VI. It is cringeworthy for a government department to sell property law this way, but I was happy to take it at its word, and asked in the comments whether they were comparing both to grand theft.

Behind the meme sits a more serious promise. In January the department's own blog told readers: "If you already own a leasehold flat, you won't be left behind." The promise rests on making conversion much easier. Easier is not the same as affordable. To borrow the meme's terms: if you want the new game, you pay for it. That is roughly where existing leaseholders now stand.

I support commonhold as the default for new flats. Nobody buying a flat should need a third-party freeholder in order to own a home in a shared building, and the Bill would end that for most new flats from around 2031. But I live under leasehold, as do millions of people wondering what this Bill does for them. It does give us something real: forfeiture, the threat of losing a home over a breach of the lease, is to be abolished and replaced with a court-led system. Beyond that, my honest answer is: less than the headlines suggest, later than they imply, and with the cost of the way out left almost entirely to residents.

Ministers have measured the problem. They examined how conversion could be financed and modelled the loss to ground rent investors. Having measured it, they declined to fund a way out for existing residents and left the key dates open.

A new model, not a way out

Commonhold for new flats happens by law. Commonhold for existing flats happens only if the residents organise it, pay for it and see it through. The Bill makes commonhold the usual model for new buildings, while existing buildings must go through a separate, collective conversion. Nothing changes for an existing leaseholder automatically.

Commonhold for new flats happens by law. Commonhold for existing flats happens only if the residents organise it, pay for it and see it through.

In most blocks, conversion means buying the freehold first. If the residents do not already own it and the freeholder will not cooperate, they generally have to acquire it through collective enfranchisement, paying valuation fees, legal costs and a premium to the freeholder, as the government's own analysis sets out.

The Bill lowers the participation threshold from unanimity to 50%. In a block of eight flats, that is four households around a kitchen table. In a block of 200, it means signing up leaseholders of at least 100 flats, showing that at least 134 are held by qualifying long leaseholders, and then raising the money and managing a purchase that involves valuation, title, mixed tenures and shared estate facilities. Anyone who has tried to get 100 neighbours to agree on anything will know which block is more likely to get there.

The right to manage shows the difference between a legal route and an open one. It lets qualifying leaseholders take over the running of their building without buying the freehold, and the 2024 Act made it easier from March 2025. Both require residents to organise. Conversion also requires them to acquire the freehold and find the money to pay for it. The remaining Law Commission reforms to the right to manage are still a commitment for this Parliament, not law.

Mixed tenure can put both routes out of residents' reach. I live in a mixed-tenure development in Aldgate, east London, where just over a third of the flats are shared ownership and the rest are rented from the housing association. Whether a building like ours meets the legal tests turns on technical questions about who holds which lease. The courts have held that shared owners with long leases count for the right to manage whether or not they have staircased, and that a housing association holding a long lease of rented flats can itself be the qualifying tenant of them. But eligibility is not control. Both the right to manage and a freehold purchase need participants holding at least half the flats, and the residents who own leases in our building hold about a third. Whatever the legal answer, neither route can start from our side without the landlord. The decision to leave leasehold belongs to the landlord, not the residents. For many housing association leaseholders, the new model will be something that happens to other people.

The decision to leave leasehold belongs to the landlord, not the residents.

Paying twice

Leaseholders have already paid for their homes once. To reach the model the government now describes as the better form of ownership, they are being asked to pay again.

Some fears are misplaced. A participating leaseholder's existing mortgage is intended to move across to the new commonhold title, so conversion does not mean a hundred new mortgage applications. The problem is the freehold premium. The Law Commission proposed government equity loans, repaid when a flat is sold, so that nobody would be priced out of converting. The government rejected that, saying it did not believe it "would be an appropriate use of taxpayer money", and it accepts that commercial lending for conversion cannot be relied on in the short term. So the money has to come from residents' savings, or from borrowing that does not yet exist.

The first movers carry the most. Neighbours who do not join keep their leases, and under the proposed route the former freeholder keeps the freehold of their flats, so the initial group does not have to buy out every holdout. But the people who go first still pay the shared acquisition and professional costs up front. The government has declined to create a mechanism for sharing those costs retrospectively, saying it would be complex and could slow conversions.

Later joiners will face their own purchase costs, so waiting is not a free ride. But the residents who organise and take the risk carry the shared costs that open the door for everyone else, with no general right to recover them.

Those who stay out are not left as they were, either. They become members of the commonhold association with a vote on the budget, but they lose the right to challenge a charge once it has been billed, or to be consulted on major works. A resident who cannot afford to buy in swaps a legal remedy for one vote among many.

I understand why the Treasury does not want to lend. But a government that calls leasehold feudal cannot then treat the way out as a private matter for each building to finance.

The legal right to buy a way out is of limited comfort to the resident who cannot afford the ticket.

The value the Bill removes

The £250 cap on existing ground rents, falling to a peppercorn after 40 years, is the Bill's headline relief. It does real good for some people. The government says it could benefit up to one million leaseholders, mainly those paying more than £250 or facing escalating increases, and it should help where a ground rent has made a flat hard to sell or mortgage. Anyone who says it helps nobody is wrong.

It also removes a great deal of value from people who bought ground rents lawfully. The government's final impact assessment puts the lifetime transfer from freeholders to leaseholders at £13.5 billion to £16.8 billion in present-value terms, up from £10 billion to £12.7 billion in the March draft. That is a modelled figure, sensitive to its assumptions, not a sum that changes hands next year. But it is a large, measurable loss, and pension funds are among the investors who carry it.

I am uneasy about Parliament cutting the value of existing investments by statute, and I say that as a leaseholder. Ground rent investors, like any investors, took on regulatory risk, and Parliament is entitled to act. There is a strong case for intervening against the worst escalating ground rents, and whether this transfer is fair is a political judgement. But the scale matters, and ministers should call it what it is: a loss imposed by law on one group to benefit another, not a tidying-up exercise. It is also different in kind from enfranchisement, where a freeholder who is bought out receives a premium.

The bigger bill stays where it was

For most leaseholders in flats, ground rent is not the largest cost of leasehold. The service charge is. In the English Housing Survey for 2023 to 2024, leaseholders in flats who paid a service charge reported a mean of £1,857 a year (median £1,500). Those who paid ground rent reported a mean of £343 (median £160). The two groups are not identical, so these are illustrative averages rather than one household's bills. But the difference in scale is hard to miss.

So why has ground rent had so much of the political attention? My suspicion is that it is the one leasehold cost a government can cut with a single clause, at no cost to itself. Service charges cannot be fixed that way. They need disclosure that landlords actually comply with, and consequences when they do not.

I know how far apart those two things can be. In August 2026 my landlord, Peabody, pleaded guilty to an offence under section 25 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, in a private prosecution I brought after it failed to provide a summary of service-charge costs that residents are entitled to request. That duty has been law since 1985. There was a consequence for ignoring it, but only because a resident went to the magistrates' court to get one. The 2024 Act adds a tribunal route: the First-tier Tribunal will be able to order compliance with the new duties and award damages of up to £5,000. Whether that works better than leaving residents to prosecute is something we will only find out in practice.

The reforms that do touch service charges sit outside this Bill and arrive in stages. From 1 April 2027, leaseholders get a right to request service-charge and management information, and new rules on landlords' litigation costs begin. From 1 November 2027, insurance commissions can no longer be recovered through the service charge, and private landlords must issue prescribed demands, standard statements of account with a qualified accountant's report, and annual reports.

Housing association leaseholders get the regime later and in a thinner form. Under the regulations, social landlords come in from 1 November 2028. Instead of the statement of account private landlords must provide, they give leaseholders a simpler expenditure report, with no requirement for a qualified accountant to check it, although some leases may still require audited accounts. The rules also bite only on accounting periods ending on or after that date. For a block whose service charge year ends in March, the first period caught ends in March 2029, so the first report would arrive later still.

The government puts the delay down to the complexity of social landlords' systems. That runs the wrong way. Complex systems are exactly where leaseholders most need standard, independently checked accounts. It matters beyond leaseholders. Housing benefit and universal credit help pay many social tenants' service charges, and the Department for Work and Pensions has confirmed it does not distinguish them from core rent in those claims. When SHAC put 225 freedom of information requests to councils, it found that almost none check whether the services they pay for actually exist. Public money helps pay these landlords' service charges, yet they face the lightest checks under the new rules.

Shared owners: the residents the Bill sees least clearly

Shared owners show the gaps most clearly. The National Audit Office's March 2026 investigation found that the government cannot currently say how quickly shared owners increase their share, does not record why households leave, and cannot tell whether a sale was a distressed one. It also confirmed that, whatever share they own, shared owners pay all of the service charge. Someone who owns 30% of a flat can be paying 100% of the service charge allocated to it.

Commonhold keeps shared ownership: shared ownership leases are permitted inside a commonhold. In a new-build commonhold, the shared owner will generally hold the vote, while the provider keeps the vote on costs it bears during the initial repair period. In a converted block, the existing provider decides whether to give shared owners membership rights at all. The government says its policy is to put shared owners first as the main voting party. In a converted block, that priority is at the landlord's discretion.

To be fair, the government has not ignored shared ownership. On 26 October 2026 it made a written statement raising the income caps, starting a second phase of improvement work that includes a review of buybacks, and promising to "significantly improve our data quality and collection". What the statement does not contain is a date for any of it.

Parliament had offered one, in a separate bill. In July the House of Lords added a clause to the Social Housing Bill requiring a review of shared ownership and staircasing within a year of the law passing. The government opposed it, and it came out when MPs on the Commons committee debated it on 27 October, the day after the written statement. My criticism is not that ministers have promised nothing. It is that they removed the one dated, measurable duty to review the model, and replaced it with a promise nobody can hold them to.

Relief without a date

The abolition of marriage value is already law, in the 2024 Act. This Bill does not abolish it; it fixes the problems that have stopped the valuation provisions coming into force. The government intends to commence them "as soon as practicable following Royal Assent". There is no date.

The ground rent cap is the same. The select committee wanted a fixed deadline of two months after Royal Assent. The government declined, keeping a power to commence by regulations, and says it aims to bring the cap in six months after Royal Assent, which it expects to be in 2028. It says it cannot commit to a fixed timetable for the Bill as a whole. Commonhold itself is expected around 2029 and the ban on new leasehold flats around 2031.

Dates are not a detail. A leaseholder with a short lease has to decide whether to extend now, sell, borrow or join a freehold purchase. An investor has to value an asset the Bill is about to cut. Neither can plan around a commitment with no date attached. I accept that a rigid date could cause problems if the regulations are not ready. The answer is a statutory target date with a narrowly defined power to extend it, the reasons reported to Parliament, and a revised date set at the same time. If ministers are confident in their timetable, they should be willing to write it down.

Who pays for the past?

On this Bill, freeholders pay through the ground rent cap, a cost the government has at least measured. Existing leaseholders pay through freehold premiums, professional fees, service charges that remain largely as they were, and years of waiting. Shared owners pay all of the service charge on homes they only partly own, with no dated review of the model they bought into. The Treasury has declined to pay for the way out.

I support the destination. I want ministers to account for the journey: to put target dates in the Bill; to offer a funded route to conversion for large and mixed-tenure blocks, including those where the tenure mix shuts residents out; to collect proper data on shared ownership and commit to a dated assessment; and to make service-charge transparency work whether or not a building converts, without making housing association leaseholders wait an extra year.

The new flat buyer should get commonhold without a fight. The existing leaseholder should not need a campaign, a new loan and a decade of promises to get a fair deal.

Disclosure: I am a shared owner in a mixed-tenure housing association development in east London and chair its residents' association. I brought the private prosecution described above, and I am lead applicant in ongoing First-tier Tribunal proceedings about service charges in my building. My building is also one of the case studies in the SHAC investigation cited above.
