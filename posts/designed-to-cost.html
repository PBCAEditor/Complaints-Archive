Designed to cost

Peabody Complaints Archive · Published 28 November 2026

The people who decide how affordable housing is built are rarely those who pay to run it. Taxpayers meet some of the bill through the benefits system, but government cannot say how much it spends on service charges.

Every affordable home is paid for out of four budgets. A developer pays to build it. A landlord pays to own and maintain it. The people who live in it pay to run it, through the service charge. And the taxpayer, through the benefits system, meets part of those running costs for residents who cannot meet them alone. Only the first two budgets are in the room when the building is designed, and they often belong to the same organisation.

The taxpayer budget is bigger than it looks. Most social renting households, 63% according to the latest English Housing Survey, now receive housing support, and DWP forecasts spending of £38.7 billion on housing benefits in 2026-27. Part of that pays for service charges. How large a part, nobody knows: in April 2024 DWP told Parliament it could not identify the figure in either Housing Benefit or Universal Credit.

This is significant because service charges, unlike rents, are not subject to the same control. Social rents are set by formula and capped. Service charges are shaped instead by decisions made before anyone moves in: how a building is designed, what goes into it and what it needs to keep running. This analysis looks at who makes those decisions, who pays for them, and why the one official rule linking the two stops short of the homes where public money is most exposed.

Four budgets, one building

None of this requires bad faith. Each of those budgets is managed sensibly on its own terms. The problem is that they belong to different people with different priorities, and the people who pay to run a building have no say in how it is built.

A developer, whether a private firm or a housing association's own development arm, has to deliver homes for less than they will sell or be valued for. That is what developers do, and there is nothing wrong with it.

For buyers, running costs barely entered the calculation, because they judged homes on price and mortgage payments. Lenders are required to count the service charge in their affordability checks, but the rules test future interest rate rises and say nothing specific about future service-charge rises. The market has begun to react only recently: Hamptons found in March that 37% of flats for sale had service charges above 1% of their value, up from 29% in 2020, and that some lenders now exclude such flats. Where running costs have slowed sales of new homes, some providers have responded by paying them for a while: PCA found L&Q offering around a year's service charges and Notting Hill Genesis contributing towards rent, service charge and mortgage payments. Those incentives expire; the service charge does not. And by the time the market reacts, the building has long been specified, and the lasting cost falls on residents, not on the people who designed it.

A housing association as landlord inherits the building and cares most about the costs it carries itself. For social rented homes it usually keeps the structure and the repairing obligations, so it has every reason to want durable roofs, walls and windows. Costs it can recover through the service charge are different. Social rents are capped; service charges are not, beyond a government expectation that providers will endeavour to keep increases within CPI plus 1%. That makes recoverable charges the natural place for cost pressure to collect. It is an incentive, not proof of what any landlord does, but it points the wrong way. Many housing associations develop their own homes, so the developer and the landlord are often the same organisation, and the split runs between two budgets inside one body.

The people who pay the service charge arrive last. Shared owners and other leaseholders pay their charges in full, whatever share of the home they own. Social tenants pay whatever housing support does not cover. The taxpayer pays the eligible charges of those who claim. None of them is consulted at the design stage.

A familiar problem

The industry has recognised this problem for nearly two decades. In 2014 a study published by the Greater London Authority, citing a 2007 Design for Homes report on high-density housing, recommended that service charges be minimised early in design, that planning briefs specify acceptable service charge levels, and that residual land calculations take account of their capitalised cost. The Mayor's Service Charges Charter, published in December 2021, says the design stage is "crucial to the performance of the service charge regime" and asks developers and providers to pay attention to the longevity of components. Homes England's 2026 guidance for shared ownership repeats much of that wording almost word for word.

The problem has been identified, studied and incorporated into guidance. What has not demonstrably changed is the financial incentive facing the people who specify the building.

Homes England agrees, for shared owners

Homes England, which funds affordable housing outside London, accepts the diagnosis. Since January 2026 its Capital Funding Guide has told providers of shared ownership homes under the new Social and Affordable Homes Programme that they "must consider the affordability of service charges when designing homes". The guide explains that affordability and apportionment flow from the built fabric, which is hard to change once a scheme is occupied, and that, where appropriate and practical, providers should consider designs that cost more at the outset but reduce what leaseholders pay later.

The duty sits in the Shared Ownership chapter and nowhere else. The Housing for Rent chapter, which governs Social Rent and Affordable Rent, contains no equivalent. Its only mention of service charges is the rule that Affordable Rent, including service charges, may be set at up to 80% of local market rent.

That is where most of the money is going. The government's £39 billion programme for 2026-36 aims to deliver at least 60% of its homes as Social Rent. Under the Rent Standard 2026, those rents are set exclusive of service charges, which sit on top. Households starting a social tenancy are also more likely than average to need help: government lettings data show that 83% of households in a new social letting in 2024/25 received some form of housing-related benefit. That figure covers all new lettings, not only newly built homes, but it indicates who the first tenants of the programme's homes are likely to be.

So the only design-stage service charge duty in Homes England's guide covers the tenure where the taxpayer is least exposed, and is absent from the Social and Affordable Rent homes the programme is built around.

Where the cost lands

Shared owners and leaseholders generally carry both kinds of cost: the running of services and, through the service charge and sinking fund, the repair and replacement of the building. New-model shared ownership leases typically include a ten-year initial repair period, during which the landlord pays for essential structural and external repairs not covered by the building warranty. In practice it overlaps that warranty and covers the decade when a new building needs least. It also sets a horizon. Where a provider's direct repair liability ends after ten years, its financial incentive to invest in durability beyond that period may weaken. The risk is that the period of financial responsibility, rather than the expected lifetime of the building, becomes the relevant investment horizon.

In social rent, the running costs of communal services are charged to tenants through the service charge. Replacement costs sometimes are too: L&Q's service charge policy, for example, lists component replacement costs among the items tenants' service charges may include. DWP's Universal Credit guidance gives what it calls an exhaustive list of eligible charges, covering the provision, maintenance and repair of communal facilities such as lifts.

Eligibility for replacement costs and reserve fund contributions depends on the nature and purpose of the charge and the applicable benefit rules. It cannot be assumed that all such expenditure is covered by housing support, and where it is not, the cost falls on tenants' own incomes.

For the taxpayer, the exposure is mainly to buildings that are expensive to run: heavy reliance on concierge or security staff, complex access control, high communal energy use, cleaning that needs specialist access. Public money can pay twice: once as capital grant through Homes England or the Greater London Authority, and again through DWP when eligible running costs are met by housing support.

The economics of cheap boots

Terry Pratchett's Sam Vimes explained the economics with boots. A good pair costs fifty dollars and lasts ten years; a cheap pair costs ten and lets the water in after a season or two, so the man who can only afford cheap boots spends a hundred dollars over the decade and still has wet feet. In housing development, the problem is more complicated than Vimes' boots. The person choosing the cheaper pair is not necessarily the person who will have to buy the replacements.

A cheaper specification does not remove a cost. It moves it from the development budget, where it would have been paid once, to the service charge, where it is paid every year for as long as the building stands, long after the people who made the choice have moved on to the next scheme. And it grows: average service charges have risen by more than half over the past decade, outstripping inflation.

That trade-off cannot be weighed when one side of it is invisible, and it is. Homes England can see the grant a cheaper specification saves; nobody can see what DWP pays as a result. In June 2026, Sir Stephen Timms, answering for DWP, said its verification is designed to establish what a claimant is liable to pay under the tenancy, and that the department does not collect data on payments for services that were not provided or were billed above the landlord's actual cost.

Government scrutinises individual benefit entitlements, yet cannot separately account for the amount of public money spent meeting residents' service charges. Without that information, it cannot properly assess whether decisions made during affordable housing development represent value for the taxpayer over the lifetime of a building.

The checks that do exist test eligibility, not value. DWP flags charges that look excessive and can refer them for independent determination, but its guidance expects referrals to be the exception rather than routine. Councils, which administer Housing Benefit, are responsible for assessing eligible service charges. Asked in January 2026 how it checks their legitimacy, Rushcliffe Borough Council confirmed that it requires supporting evidence from providers of exempt accommodation but undertakes no equivalent analysis for housing associations. The Social Housing Action Campaign (SHAC), drawing on 225 Freedom of Information requests, reported in April that councils check whether charges are eligible but almost none check whether the services actually exist.

Even a thorough audit of reasonableness would not solve the problem described here. A service charge can be accurately calculated, legally recoverable and reasonable for the building as built, and still be higher than it needed to be because of a decision taken before anyone signed a lease or a tenancy. The law on reasonableness already exists. What is missing is accountability at the point where running costs are created.

What gets checked at design stage

Homes England audits a sample of funded schemes each year. Its 2026-27 checklist for strategic partnership schemes asks auditors to confirm that shared ownership rents and service charges were set in line with the guide at sale, at rent increases and on resale, and checks how sinking fund contributions were calculated. We could not find a question asking whether service charge affordability was considered at design stage. That is partly timing: the checklist's notes refer to homes from the 2021-26 programme, designed before the duty existed. But a duty to consider, with no requirement to record what was considered, will leave an auditor little to test.

Even where a breach is found, residents would not know: the audits are carried out by auditors the provider appoints, on a sample of schemes, and Homes England treats the reports as confidential between itself and the provider. The duty sits in the funder's rules rather than in residents' leases, so the people who pay the service charge have no way to enforce it.

Partial answers

Some landlords are responding. In July 2025 L&Q announced that it would merge its property services and development directorates to manage the quality of homes across the full lifetime of each property. Bringing the people who maintain buildings closer to those who design them should help lessons about lifts, plant and access reach the drawing board. But reorganisation changes who talks to whom, not who pays. L&Q's Service Charges and Sinking Funds Policy, approved in May 2026, frames cost reduction as procurement, efficiency and value-for-money reviews, and does not address new-build design. Its design standards may sit elsewhere, but the published policy is silent on the decisions that set charges in the first place. A restructure is a promise, not yet a result.

Parliament had a chance to look wider. Clause 13 of the Social Housing Bill [HL], added in the Lords, would have required a statutory review of shared ownership. On 27 October 2026 the Commons committee removed it by nine votes to five, the minister arguing that it would duplicate improvement work already under way.

What should change

Better data and guidance will help, but neither changes who gains from a construction saving and who pays for it later. Guidance tells providers what to consider; legislation can make them answer for their decisions.

PCA's view is that the gap is legislative. Every new affordable housing development, regardless of how it is funded or procured, should be subject to a mandatory whole-life affordability assessment. That assessment should justify significant specification choices, identify the expected lifespan of major components and forecast service charges at completion and after five, ten and fifteen years. Forecasts should be published and subsequently tested against actual expenditure.

But forecasting alone changes little. Providers should have a financial interest in the accuracy of those assessments. Where premature component failure or excessive running costs arise from avoidable deficiencies in design, specification or installation, the cost should fall on those responsible rather than automatically passing to residents.

One mechanism would be to require providers to fund the anticipated replacement of major components over their declared operational lifespan, meeting the additional cost themselves where those components fail prematurely because of decisions within their control. Declaring a shorter lifespan increases the funding required from the outset; declaring an unrealistically long one creates a liability later. Either way, durability becomes a financial consideration rather than merely a design aspiration.

This need not make providers liable for every increase in expenditure. Inflation and regulatory changes are different matters, and maintenance failures are a separate liability. The objective is to place financial responsibility with the organisation best able to control the risk.

DWP and MHCLG should also record the service charge component of housing support, so that capital funding and subsequent benefit expenditure can finally be considered together. Government cannot meaningfully assess the lifetime value of its investment in affordable housing while part of its expenditure remains unquantified.

Homes England need not wait for legislation. It could extend its shared ownership design requirement to rented housing, require documented assessments and make compliance an explicit part of its audit regime. Funding conditions, however, cannot provide universal coverage. A statutory requirement would apply regardless of the route through which affordable housing is delivered.

The legislation should prohibit providers from directly recovering the costs of compliance, or liabilities arising from their own design failures, through service charges or additional grant claims. Otherwise, an obligation intended to protect residents and taxpayers risks becoming another expense for them to meet.

There are trade-offs. Higher construction costs could mean fewer homes under a fixed capital budget. New liabilities could affect development margins and land values. Appraisals could become exercises in optimistic forecasting or defensive overestimation. Nor should lower service charges be achieved by compromising safety, accessibility or necessary maintenance.

Those are questions a comprehensive review of affordable housing should examine. The proposed statutory review of shared ownership would have provided an opportunity to investigate some of them, but the financial exposure of the taxpayer makes the case for looking beyond that tenure.

The purpose of intervention should not be to persuade developers to disregard their own financial interests. It should be to establish a framework in which their financial interests and the long-term affordability of the homes they build are better aligned.

Affordable at completion

Affordable housing is judged by what it costs to build and what it costs to buy or rent on the first day. Residents live with what it costs to run for decades afterwards, and so, through the benefits system, does the taxpayer. Every party involved in building those homes is doing what its own budget asks. Until someone is made to answer for the budget that comes afterwards, homes will go on being affordable at completion and costly for the rest of their lives.
