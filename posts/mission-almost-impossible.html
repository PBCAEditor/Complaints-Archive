Mission (Almost) Impossible: The Librarian Taking Her Landlord to Criminal Court

By David Wood · Published 19 November 2026

A shared owner in outer east London has taken one question to the Housing Ombudsman, the Property Tribunal and now the Magistrates' Court. Her new book sets out what each route has cost her.

The sums that did not add up: Dead Reckoning

Olivia Virag bought a 30% share in a new two-bedroom flat in 2020. The block has 46 homes and sits on the eastern edge of Greater London. Her landlord is a council-owned housing company, which her book does not name.

For two years she filed the service charge letters without checking them. Like most of us, she assumed they were fine. She did not move in looking for a dispute.

When she did go back through them, the columns didn't match the totals, and one letter described the annual figure as monthly. You didn't need to be a mathematician to see that the numbers didn't add up.

The following year's letter had the same odd accounting. That letter, and the one after it, carried her flat number and her neighbour's name. When she asked for a corrected copy in 2023, it arrived with her name and different figures.

By her count, five of the twelve estimates and final accounts she has received show a total that does not match the figures printed above it. She reproduces them in her book.

On 8 October 2023 she put a complaint in writing. She asked a simple question: what am I paying for?

Two years in a queue: Ghost Protocol

She took the complaint to the Housing Ombudsman in January 2024 and sent everything at once: the errors, the services, the unanswered emails. She suspects the mountain of evidence was a mistake of presentation.

Through 2024 she kept the Ombudsman updated and received acknowledgements. No investigation began. By 2025 she had stopped waiting and was preparing a Tribunal application.

In March 2026 the Ombudsman told her it would not investigate, because the dispute was now before the Tribunal. She had gone to the Tribunal because the Ombudsman had not acted. The Ombudsman then closed the complaint because she had gone to the Tribunal.

Asking under statute: Should You Choose to Accept It

In 2025 she found sections 21 and 22 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985. The first lets a leaseholder require a written summary of service charge costs. The second gives access to the records behind it.

On 29 May 2025 she asked for the summary for 2024-25. Sixty-one days later she was sent a breakdown for a different year. The right one arrived on 30 October, 154 days after the request and a month past the statutory deadline. Her log records at least thirteen contacts chasing it.

She also asked for two earlier years. For 2020-21 she eventually received two spreadsheets, both headed as actual costs. One put caretaking at £14,959.24 and the other at £37,545.07. Gardening was £6,674.40 in one and nil in the other. She says nothing told her which to rely on.

A year on the form of the question: This Case Will Self-Destruct

She applied to the First-tier Tribunal on 15 October 2025, and soon discovered what it costs to challenge a service charge.

The legal advice she priced cost around £500 an hour, with figures near £20,000 mentioned for representation. She works in a library and earns about £15 an hour. The charges she was disputing came to roughly £10,000, so paying for representation made no sense. Determined, she decided to represent herself.

More than a year later, the Tribunal has not decided a single charge. The time has gone on how her case should be set out. "All I wanted to know was what my money paid for," she writes.

Her first statement worked through the accounts year by year. The landlord's lawyers said they could not understand it. A hearing became a case management conference instead. There she learned that the Tribunal wanted a Scott Schedule, a table with one row for each disputed charge. A template had come with her first papers. She had taken it for a suggestion, and she calls that her own error.

In June the landlord applied to have the whole case struck out. Its solicitor's statement suggested she had handed the work to AI. It also described the proceedings as a "cost-free exercise" for her.

The remark lands in a live argument. The Financial Ombudsman Service has said this year that long, inaccurate AI-drafted complaints are slowing its work. A former Lord Chancellor, Robert Buckland, has urged judges to use costs orders against what he called "AI slop" from people representing themselves. Against that, the Master of the Rolls and the Lady Chief Justice have each said the same tools can help such people put their case.

The relevant questions are whether submissions are accurate, relevant and manageable. Ms Virag says she used AI to understand legal language and improve her English. According to her account, the landlord challenged the length and structure of her submissions without identifying invented cases or fictitious law. The Tribunal nevertheless required her case to be reshaped.

The strike-out application was declined, and the same order turned the two-day final hearing listed for October into another case management conference. That conference lasted four hours. Every disputed item under £100 was removed from her schedule.

She was also given to understand that the 23 statements she had gathered from neighbours may count for little unless the neighbours attend. She had seven days to rebuild the schedule. The landlord was given more than three months to respond.

Outside the hearing room, settlement was raised. She says she was told it would come with confidentiality, and she declined on that ground.

The Tribunal's own rules say that dealing with a case fairly includes avoiding unnecessary formality and making sure the parties can take part fully. Ms Virag's account shows how that works in practice. She describes the first hearing as humiliating and the second judge as kinder. After the first, she decided that "First-tear Tribunal" was the more accurate spelling.

Informality helps most when both sides are amateurs, and here the parties are not equal. A self-represented resident is expected to lock horns with a seasoned professional who has just come from one case and will follow this appearance with another.

The Magistrates' Court: Fallout

Failing to answer a section 21 request without reasonable excuse is a criminal offence under section 25 of the same Act. The maximum fine is £2,500.

In August, Peabody pleaded guilty to that offence at Thames Magistrates' Court and was fined £1,000, in a private prosecution I brought. Ms Virag writes that reading about the case showed her a route she had not known was open.

On 29 August 2026 she requested the summary for 2025-26. Her landlord acknowledged the request and said it expected to respond by 30 September, the statutory deadline. She says that by mid-October nothing had arrived. She sent her application to the court on 26 October. A summons was issued two days later.

A summons is not a finding. The court has not decided whether an offence was committed, and the words "without reasonable excuse" leave the landlord room to answer. Its side of this account has not been heard, in court or here.

Where it stands: Mission Incomplete

At the Tribunal, the case drags on. The landlord's disclosure is due on 19 November and its full response in January. The final hearing is listed for May 2027. At the Magistrates' Court, the prosecution awaits its first hearing.

A separate complaint, about the roof of her building, is still open with the Ombudsman. She fears the wait will be longer after the IT security incident the Ombudsman reported in October.

Her book, Mission (Almost) Impossible: A Beginner's Guide to Housing Battles, runs to 100 pages. The first half tells this story in far more detail than a summary can. The second half is a practical guide to the three routes, with timetables, forms, fees and a frank list of what she would do differently. Readers who reach the section 25 chapter and want the procedure step by step will find it in this site's guide to private prosecution.

Her argument is that none of the rights she relied on is new. Each was already on the statute book. What she questions is how many residents have the money, time and stamina to make one work.

The story told in the book is not yet finished. I eagerly await Mission (Almost) Impossible II.

Disclosure: Ms Virag's book cites my private prosecution of Peabody as the prompt for her own, and she agreed to this feature. Her landlord is not Peabody.
