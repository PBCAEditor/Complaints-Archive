Editor's note. This is written evidence submitted to the House of Commons Public Bill Committee on the Social Housing Bill [HL] on 18 September 2026, and published by Parliament as memorandum SHB01. It is reproduced here with the building name and the name of the residents' association removed. "The Association" refers to the recognised residents' association that made the submission.

The paragraph numbering is as submitted, so any paragraph can be cited as it appears in the parliamentary record. The version published by Parliament lists its sources as citations without hyperlinks; the links below are those supplied with the original submission, each checked on 15 November 2026.

Written evidence to the Public Bill Committee on the Social Housing Bill [HL], clause 13 (review of shared ownership and staircasing). Submitted by David Wood, Chair of a recognised residents' association ("the Association") within the meaning of section 29 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985.

About the submitter and scope of this evidence

1.The Association is a recognised residents' association for the purposes of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 at a mixed-tenure development in East London. It represents shared ownership leaseholders and has direct experience of service-charge accounting, statutory information requests, complaints, Ombudsman processes and enforcement against a registered provider.

2.This evidence is submitted by David Wood as Chair of the Association. It addresses clause 13 of the Social Housing Bill [HL], as brought from the Lords.[11] It does not seek simply to repeat the 2024 Commons inquiry. It considers whether the gaps identified then have since been filled, drawing on the 2024/25 English Housing Survey, the National Audit Office's 2026 investigation, SHAC's 2026 survey and the Association's completed enforcement case.

Summary and recommendations

3.Parliament should retain clause 13. Shared ownership is a large, growing and publicly supported housing product, but government cannot yet answer basic questions about its long-term affordability, the movement of purchasers through the tenure, or the circumstances in which residents can sell and move on.

4.Existing departmental work is not an adequate substitute for clause 13. It has no equivalent statutory scope, 12-month deadline, publication duty, requirement to report to Parliament or requirement to state what action will follow. The need for a formal review is not confined to residents and campaigners. In September 2026, the Chartered Institute of Housing called for a comprehensive, evidence-based review of the tenure, supported by longitudinal tracking of affordability, staircasing, resale and tenure outcomes.[12] Clause 13 provides the statutory mechanism needed to ensure that this work is completed and its conclusions are published.

5.The review should measure affordability using total unavoidable housing costs, not rent and mortgage payments alone. It should include service charges, estate and management charges, insurance, reserve-fund contributions, repairs and the transaction costs of staircasing or sale.

6.The review should measure more than full staircasing. Sustainable partial ownership, equity accumulated, arrears, repossession, resident satisfaction, the ability to remortgage, the time and cost of sale, and any loss of equity on exit are also material outcomes.

7.The review should cover existing households whose leases were granted under earlier model terms, not only purchasers of new products. Most current shared owners will not benefit from every protection attached to newer leases.

8.The Association supports clause 13 and does not make that support conditional on amendment. The present wording, however, concentrates on staircasing towards full ownership. It does not expressly require the review to examine whether shared owners can sell and leave when full ownership is unaffordable, inappropriate or no longer their objective. To close that gap, we suggest inserting after subsection (2)(f):

"(g) the operation and effectiveness of routes by which shared ownership leaseholders may resell their interest or otherwise exit the scheme, including nomination periods, valuation requirements, fees, buyback, reverse staircasing, subletting and homes that are difficult to sell or mortgage; and"

9.Subsection (2)(f) refers to transparency, but transparency is not achieved merely by creating a right to information. The review should also examine whether residents can exercise that right, understand the information provided and obtain an effective remedy when a landlord fails to comply. We therefore suggest adding paragraph (h) alongside paragraph (g):

"(h) the accessibility and effectiveness of information rights, complaints, redress and enforcement."

10.The review should be informed by those who use, finance, provide and regulate shared ownership, and its action statement should include an implementation timetable. We therefore suggest inserting after subsection (3):

"(4) In conducting the review, the Secretary of State must consult:

(a) shared ownership leaseholders and persons appearing to the Secretary of State to represent them;

(b) registered providers and mortgage lenders;

(c) the Regulator of Social Housing and the Housing Ombudsman; and

(d) such other persons as the Secretary of State considers appropriate.

(5) The statement under subsection (3) must include a timetable for implementing any proposed actions."

11.If the Committee does not amend the clause, Ministers should at least commit on the record to including these matters in the review's terms of reference and to providing an implementation timetable in the statement of proposed action.

Why a statutory review is necessary

12.The English Housing Survey estimates that shared ownership grew from 161,000 households in 2019/20 to 252,000 in 2024/25. The National Audit Office recorded 20,353 new shared ownership homes in 2024/25, approximately 11 per cent of new-build supply.[1][2] Shared ownership is therefore not a marginal product. Public grant, planning obligations, provider capital and household savings continue to be committed to it.

13.The Lords debate identified gaps in national evidence about ongoing affordability, staircasing, equity loss, mortgageability and resale during nomination periods.[3] At Commons Second Reading, Ministers said that a statutory review was unnecessary because the Government was already considering data and long-term affordability and would report back later.[4]

14.That response does not answer the case for clause 13. Departmental activity can be changed, narrowed or delayed without parliamentary approval. Clause 13 provides a settled scope, a deadline, publication and an action statement laid before Parliament. Those are safeguards of accountability, not administrative duplication.

15.The National Audit Office investigation is important, but it is not the review required by clause 13. The NAO did not assess value for money and did not make recommendations. It found that historic data are incomplete, that government does not fully understand customer journeys and that redress is complex.[2] Those findings establish the need for the review rather than remove it.

16.The 2024 Commons inquiry had already called for better evidence about long-term affordability and staircasing.[7] The case for clause 13 is not that Parliament failed to diagnose the problem. It is that evidence published two years later shows that core gaps remain: the principal affordability statistic still excludes service charges, historic data remain incomplete and government still does not fully understand customer journeys.[1][2] In September 2026, the Chartered Institute of Housing similarly called for a comprehensive, evidence-based review of the tenure, including longitudinal tracking, affordability trajectories and resale outcomes.[12] Clause 13 would convert repeated diagnosis into a time-limited duty to reach and publish conclusions.

17.A statutory review need not pre-judge reform or destabilise supply. Clause 13 changes no lease, grant condition or funding term. A bounded 12-month examination can assess lender appetite, provider finance, public subsidy and development capacity alongside consumer outcomes. Better evidence about mortgageability, service charges and resale should reduce long-term uncertainty.

Affordability must mean total housing cost

18.The principal official affordability measure is incomplete. The 2024/25 English Housing Survey reports that shared owners spend an average 23 per cent of household income on rent and mortgage payments. That is more than mortgagors at 19 per cent, but less than social renters at 27 per cent and private renters at 33 per cent. The measure excludes service charges.[1]

19.The exclusion matters most for households with the least room to absorb another unavoidable cost. Among households in the bottom two income quintiles, 40 per cent of shared owners already spend at least 30 per cent of household income on rent and mortgage payments alone. The comparable figures are 36 per cent for mortgagors, 45 per cent for social renters and 71 per cent for private renters.[1] The published measure cannot show how many shared owners cross the affordability threshold once their service charge is added.

20.The same survey found that 61 per cent of shared owners considered their service charge high, compared with 46 per cent of other leaseholders. Only 39 per cent considered it fair, compared with 50 per cent of other leaseholders.[1] Those comparisons show a materially different reported experience from that of other leaseholders.

21.Complaint evidence points in the same direction. Forty-three per cent of shared owners had considered complaining to their freeholder or management company in the previous 12 months, compared with 24 per cent of other leaseholders. Among those who complained, 82 per cent of shared owners were unhappy with the response, compared with 70 per cent of leaseholders overall. Repairs were mentioned by 78 per cent of shared-owner complainants, compared with 56 per cent of other leaseholders.[1]

22.These figures do not establish support for the precise wording of clause 13, but they identify a large constituency likely to welcome an authoritative review of affordability, service charges and redress. Consultation can be conducted through normal departmental business. Clause 13 matters because it requires the Government to complete the review, publish its conclusions and state what action will follow. It is therefore capable of being a popular and readily understood consumer measure, not merely a technical data exercise.

23.Government guidance states that shared owners are normally responsible for repairs and maintenance irrespective of the share owned, subject to limited arrangements such as the initial repair period attached to newer leases.[5] The NAO similarly records that shared owners pay all service-charge costs regardless of their equity share and that service charges lack a standard format and terminology.[2]

24.These facts do not show that every charge is unreasonable. Insurance, energy, staffing, building safety and regulatory requirements can create genuine costs. They do show that a review which measures only mortgage and rent cannot answer whether the tenure remains affordable in practice.

25.The review should therefore calculate total unavoidable housing costs over time. It should distinguish initial affordability from affordability after five, ten and fifteen years, and should show the effect of rent increases, interest rates, service charges, major works, repairs and administration fees on households at different income levels.

Government cannot yet measure the staircase

26.The NAO found incomplete historic data and limited understanding of how purchasers move through shared ownership. Provider data have not always been complete, non-compliance has not routinely been escalated, and partial staircasing has not been captured consistently.[2]

27.The 2024/25 official sales release records 4,781 low-cost home-ownership sales in which ownership reached 100 per cent. That number is not a success rate for shared ownership. The category includes more than one form of low-cost home ownership and is a flow from unknown entry cohorts.[6] It cannot show what proportion of the present 252,000 shared-owner households will staircase, how long the process takes or how outcomes vary.

28.For many staircasing transactions, particularly purchases of 5 per cent or more and transactions under older lease terms, the resident must repeat many of the professional and administrative steps involved in buying a property, including valuation, legal work, mortgage arrangements and provider administration. In practical terms, the resident may pay much of the cost of buying a home again each time another share is purchased. Repeated transaction costs can therefore make incremental staircasing less affordable, not more.[2]

29.The review should use cohort data. It should follow entrants by year, provider, region, property type, initial share and lease generation, recording partial staircasing, full staircasing, remortgage, resale, arrears and repossession. Aggregate annual completions cannot reveal those pathways.

30.Full ownership should not be treated as the only successful outcome. A resident may rationally prefer stable partial ownership. The relevant question is whether the arrangement remains affordable, secure and saleable, whether equity is accumulated, and whether the household can move when its needs change.

Exit and resale are part of effectiveness

31.A housing product cannot be assessed only at entry. Household circumstances can change. A viable tenure must include a workable route out as well as a route in. The review should examine nomination periods, valuation rules, provider and agent fees, marketing restrictions, remortgaging, buyback, reverse staircasing, subletting and homes that become difficult to sell or mortgage. It should record time to sale, failed transactions, price reductions and loss of equity. The review should also identify whether a restriction that served a legitimate allocation purpose at purchase later delays or prevents a necessary move.

32.An MHCLG-funded report published in August 2026 found that fewer than 45 buyback funding bids were approved nationally over five years, that provider-reported buyback rates ranged from 0.01 to 0.063 per cent of shared ownership homes per year, and that only 19 of 65 providers surveyed published any information about buyback.[10] Those figures suggest that the principal exit route beyond open-market sale barely functions in practice. Without the data that clause 13 would require, there is no way to know whether this reflects low demand or systemic inaccessibility.

Transparency, redress and enforcement

33.The NAO describes redress as complex. Complaint handling and service issues may fall within the Housing Ombudsman's remit, while the First-tier Tribunal determines service-charge payability and reasonableness. Tribunal proceedings involve fees, procedure and potential advice costs, and registered providers are commonly legally represented.[2]

34.In 2023/24, 13.5 per cent of shared-ownership complaints investigated by the Housing Ombudsman produced an outcome in favour of the resident, compared with 8.6 per cent across other tenures. A favourable outcome was therefore around one and a half times as common in shared-ownership cases. The most common issues were property condition and complaint handling, followed by service charges.[2]

35.The same pattern appears in broader satisfaction data. Tenant Satisfaction Measures for 2024/25 recorded satisfaction of 48 per cent among low-cost home owners, the majority of whom are shared owners, compared with 71 per cent among low-cost renters.[2]

36.Transparency is not achieved merely by creating an information right. Residents must be able to obtain the information within a useful period, understand it, identify who is responsible and secure a practical remedy where the duty is not met.

37.This is particularly important where a registered provider employs a managing agent or sits below a superior landlord. The legal duty may rest with one body while the relevant records and operational systems sit with another. The review should examine whether responsibility is sufficiently clear and enforceable across that chain.

Case study: the Association's enforcement case

38.The Association began making statutory requests for service-charge information under sections 21 and 22 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 in 2023. Requests, complaints and assurances continued over an extended period. Peabody's own written review in November 2025 recorded failures to meet those obligations and inaccurate statements about whether the law had been complied with. The Association holds the relevant requests, acknowledgements, complaint responses and review.

39.A further section 21 request was made in January 2026. The statutory deadline passed without compliant information being provided. On 11 August 2026, at Thames Magistrates' Court, Peabody Trust pleaded guilty to failing without reasonable excuse to perform the duty imposed by section 21. It was fined £1,000 and ordered to pay a £400 victim surcharge. No order for costs was made. The prosecution was brought privately by a leaseholder. A certified extract from the court register and the summons are publicly identified in the Association's evidence log.[8]

40.The case is not offered as evidence of prevalence across all providers, and the Association does not ask this Committee to investigate an individual grievance. It relies on completed criminal proceedings, Peabody's written admissions and contemporaneous correspondence. It is a documented example of how opacity becomes an affordability and accountability problem; without adequate information, a resident cannot test a service charge, budget for future liability or make an informed decision about staircasing or sale.

41.The significance is not simply that a registered provider broke the law. The statutory right failed to deliver the information within the period Parliament prescribed. To obtain even a criminal sanction, an individual resident had to preserve the documentary record, identify the offence, navigate the procedure and bring a private prosecution. A transparency regime that depends on a leaseholder prosecuting a registered provider is not an effective system of enforcement. The criminal court could punish the offence but could not create an efficient continuing system of disclosure.

Wider resident evidence

42.Through engagement with the Social Housing Action Campaign and other shared-owner networks, we have encountered reports from residents of different providers describing steep and poorly explained cost increases, difficulty obtaining underlying records, responsibility being passed between landlord and agent, staircasing made unaffordable by accumulated costs, and sales delayed by provider or building issues.[9][10]

43.SHAC's 2026 online service-charge survey received 829 valid responses. Of the 809 respondents who stated their tenure, 219 were shared owners. Across respondents of all tenures who requested summary statements, half of those reported receiving only partial information, 23.2 per cent reported receiving no response, and 72.6 per cent of respondents who received any response said that it arrived outside the 30-day statutory period.[9] The survey was self-selecting, covered several tenures and was weighted towards housing-association and London respondents. It should not be treated as a national prevalence estimate. It does, however, corroborate the mechanisms identified by official evidence and by the Association's records.

44.A recurring theme is the evidential burden placed on the individual. The resident must retain demands and correspondence, reconcile changing descriptions of costs, use a complaints process, decide between Ombudsman and Tribunal routes and, in the Association's case, enforce a criminal provision. A review of transparency which excludes the accessibility and effectiveness of redress would therefore be incomplete.

Required scope and method of the review

45.The review should be co-designed with shared owners, including residents on 'legacy leases', meaning leases granted under earlier iterations of the Affordable Homes Programme and their model lease terms that remain in force for existing households. It should also include residents who have sold, tried to sell or fallen into arrears. It should not rely only on provider returns that official bodies have already found to be incomplete.

46.Its affordability measure should include mortgage, rent, service and estate charges, insurance, reserve contributions, repairs, major works and transaction fees. Results should be shown in cash terms and as a proportion of disposable household income.

47.Its outcome data should be longitudinal and published in a form that permits comparison by provider, region, initial share, property type and lease generation, while protecting personal data. Definitions should be consistent and provider non-compliance with data returns should be reported.

48.Its examination of service charges should cover standardisation, advance estimates, year-end reconciliation, apportionment, reserve funds, access to supporting records, landlord and managing-agent responsibility, and the time and cost required to obtain redress.

49.Its examination of staircasing and exit should include the full cost and duration of transactions, failed applications and sales, mortgageability, nomination periods, valuation disputes, buyback and reverse staircasing, and the impact of building safety and lease length.

50.Where a reform would shift cost from residents to providers or government, the review should identify the amount and funding route so that Parliament can weigh cost alongside the consumer harm of inaction.

51.The statement laid before Parliament should distinguish action that can be taken through guidance, funding conditions or regulation from action requiring primary or secondary legislation. It should give a named lead body and timetable for each proposed action.

Conclusion

52.The 2024 Commons inquiry diagnosed serious weaknesses in affordability, staircasing and exit. Evidence published or arising since then shows that the central information and enforcement gaps remain. The principal affordability statistic still excludes service charges, longitudinal staircasing data are incomplete and routes of redress remain fragmented.

53.The Association's case shows the practical consequence of one part of that system. Where access to information required to understand service charges culminated in a resident bringing a successful private prosecution, transparency cannot be assessed without enforcement.

54.Parliament should retain clause 13. A statutory, time-limited and published review is the minimum proportionate response to questions that remain unanswered after years of policy development and continued public investment.
