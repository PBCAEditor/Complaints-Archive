Service charge information and private prosecution: the guides

If you pay a variable service charge, the law lets you ask your landlord for a summary of the costs behind it, and to inspect the accounts and receipts. If the landlord fails to comply without a reasonable excuse, it commits a criminal offence, and you can prosecute it yourself in the magistrates' court. These guides take you through both steps, from writing the request to the first hearing in court.

They are based on a real case. In August 2026 Peabody Trust pleaded guilty to a section 25 offence after a resident prosecuted it (read about the case). The guides set out what that case taught us, with model documents you can adapt.

Written by residents, not lawyers. Please read “Before you use these guides” at the foot of this page.

The law is changing. In England, the section 22 right to inspect accounts and receipts, and prosecutions for breaching it, end on 1 April 2027. The section 21 right and its offence continue until 1 November 2027 for private landlords and 1 November 2028 for housing associations. Each guide explains what this means for you.

Requesting service charge information

Start here. How to ask your landlord for a summary of service charge costs (section 21) and to inspect the supporting accounts and receipts (section 22), with model request letters and how to work out the deadline. You can read it yourself or give it to an AI assistant; it includes instructions telling the assistant how to help you.

Use it when you want information about your service charge, or when you may want to prosecute later and need a valid request to start from.

Guide to Private Prosecutions under Section 25

The main guide. How to decide whether you have a case, apply to the magistrates' court for a summons, serve the papers on your landlord and prepare for the first hearing. It includes checklists and model documents: a completed summons application, a certificate of service, the Initial Details of the Prosecution Case, a witness statement and a written argument.

Use it when you made a valid request, the deadline has passed and the landlord has not complied. Check the six-month time limit first.

If They Plead Guilty

What happens if the landlord admits the offence at the first hearing: outlining the facts to the court, sentencing, applying for your costs and getting a formal record of the conviction.

Use it before your first hearing, because the court may sentence on the day.

If They Plead Not Guilty

A working booklet to print and bring to the first hearing. If the landlord denies the offence, the court will usually move straight on to planning the trial. The booklet helps you record what is disputed, what is agreed, who needs to give evidence and every deadline the court sets. It also includes model directions you can hand to the court.

Use it at your first hearing, whichever way you expect the plea to go.

Prosecution costs

How to claim back what the prosecution cost you if the landlord is convicted: your out-of-pocket expenses and, if the court allows it, your time. Includes a model costs schedule.

Use it from the start, so you keep a log of your time and spending, and again before the first hearing to prepare your claim.

Preparing for Trial (under construction)

Disclosure, evidence, witnesses and the trial itself. Until it is published, take legal advice if your case is heading for trial.

Before you use these guides

These guides are written by residents, not lawyers. They are information, not legal advice.

They are based on one prosecution, brought in the London magistrates' courts in 2026. Other courts may handle things differently, court procedures and forms change, and your landlord, your lease and your circumstances may not match ours. Check when each guide was last reviewed, and check anything important against the current law and court rules.

Take legal advice if you are unsure about anything, especially before you apply for a summons, if your landlord disputes the case, or if it is going to trial. The Leasehold Advisory Service (LEASE) gives free legal advice on leasehold law, including service charges (lease-advice.org). For court procedure, speak to a solicitor.

A private prosecution is a serious step. You take on the duties of a prosecutor, and in some circumstances a court can order a prosecutor to pay the defendant's costs. The main guide explains how to avoid that.

We cannot advise on individual cases. If you find an error in any guide, please tell us at PBCAEditor@gmail.com.
