This guide concerns the existing sections 21 to 25 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1985. In England they are being replaced in stages under the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. The section 22 and 23 duties, and the offence of breaching them, end on 1 April 2027. The section 21 duty and its offence continue until 1 November 2027 for private landlords and 1 November 2028 for housing associations and other social landlords. After those dates, failures go to the tribunal instead of the criminal courts. Dates for Wales have not yet been set. If the failure you want to prosecute happened before one of these dates, you may still be able to prosecute it within the usual six months, but take advice first.

Note: This companion guide should be read alongside our main Guide to Private Prosecutions under Section 25, particularly the section explaining which landlords can and cannot be prosecuted in England and Wales.

What happens after a guilty plea

If the defendant pleads guilty at the plea hearing, there will normally be no trial. The court may ask you for a brief outline of the facts of the offence.

The case summary in your IDPC has been designed for this purpose. Have a copy with you and use it as the basis of your account to the court. Use it as a prompt rather than reading the whole document aloud. You do not need to prepare a separate statement or go through all of your evidence. Keep your account short and factual. If the court wants more detail, it will ask.

Listen carefully to anything the defendant says in mitigation. If they make a factual assertion which is materially incorrect and relevant to sentence, you should draw this to the court’s attention when given the opportunity.

Sometimes a defendant pleads guilty but puts forward its own written account of the facts, called a basis of plea. Read it carefully. If it differs from your case in a way that would affect the sentence, say so. The court may then decide the disputed facts at a separate hearing before it sentences.

Sentencing

The court decides sentence. You should be ready to tell the court the maximum penalty and any facts which are relevant to the seriousness of the offence. You do not need to calculate the fine or demand a particular sentence.

The maximum penalty for a section 25 offence is a level 4 fine (currently £2,500).

The court will reduce the fine to reflect the guilty plea. For a plea at the first hearing the reduction can be up to one third; it is smaller for a later plea.

The court must also order a victim surcharge, which for a fine is currently 40 per cent of the fine. The fine and the surcharge are paid to the court, not to you. Costs, if the court awards them, are the only money that comes to you.

The court must take the defendant’s financial circumstances into account when setting the fine, so far as they are known. For a housing association or company, it can help to have its latest published accounts with you in case the court asks.

Costs

You should also make your application for prosecution costs. Your costs schedule should be prepared separately, because the appropriate amount will depend on how the case has progressed and when the guilty plea was entered. Bring copies of your completed costs schedule and any receipts or other evidence supporting the expenses claimed. Be ready to give a copy to the defendant and the court. See Prosecution costs, which includes a model costs schedule.

Obtaining a Memorandum of Conviction

You do not normally need to obtain any further document for the conviction to be valid. However, if you may need formal proof of the conviction later, you can ask the magistrates’ court for a certified copy of the Memorandum of Conviction. This is the formal court record of the conviction and sentence. A Memorandum of Conviction is the document ordinarily used to prove a magistrates’ court conviction.

Contact the court which dealt with the case and ask for a certified copy of the Memorandum of Conviction. Give them:

  • the case number
  • the defendant’s name
  • your name as prosecutor
  • the date and court of conviction
  • the offence

You can find the court’s current contact details using the GOV.UK Find a Court or Tribunal service.

The court will tell you how to pay the required fee. At the time of writing, the fee for a certified Memorandum of Conviction is £15, but check the current fee before applying. The payment process varies between courts. Follow whatever instructions the court gives you, and keep your payment confirmation: the court may need to see it before releasing the document.

Questions and support

If something unexpected happens at the hearing, or the court raises something you do not understand, you can ask for a short adjournment if you need time to take advice or think through your options.

If your case does not fit the straightforward examples in this guide, obtain legal advice.

Before you rely on this

This is not legal advice. These guides are written by residents, not lawyers. They are based on one prosecution, brought in the London magistrates' courts in 2026. Other courts may handle things differently, and court procedures and forms change. Check anything important against the current law and court rules, and take legal advice if you are unsure, especially before you apply for a summons, if your landlord disputes the case, or if it is going to trial. See also Before you use these guides.

A private prosecution is a serious step. You take on the duties of a prosecutor, and in some circumstances a court can order a prosecutor to pay the defendant's costs. The main guide and Prosecution costs explain how to avoid that.
